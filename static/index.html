Where the rainfall comes from

Open-Meteo publishes the ERA5 weather record as a daily rainfall total for any point on land. The service asks for the last ten full years, keeps the days above 1 mm, and averages them into one year.

Ten years rather than one because the total swings a lot. Around this sample map it ran from 1,057 mm to 1,858 mm over the last decade.

One caveat travels with the number. ERA5 sits on a grid about 25 km wide, so it spreads a village's rain over more days than a rain gauge in that village would. Runoff grows faster than rainfall, so the yield you get back is on the low side. Check it against the nearest gauge before you cost a design.

What the curve number means

It is one number, from 30 to 98, for how readily the ground turns rain into runoff instead of soaking it up. Low means thirsty ground: deep sand under forest sheds almost nothing. High means sealed ground: a concrete yard sheds almost all of it. It bundles up the soil type, what is growing on it and how wet it already was, because a design does not get to measure those separately.

The whole method is two lines. The curve number sets how much water the soil can still take:

S = 25400 / CN − 254, in millimetres. At CN 75 that is 85 mm.

Then each day of rain P produces runoff Q:

Q = (P − 0.2S)² / (P − 0.8S), and nothing at all until P passes 0.2S. At CN 75 the first 17 mm of a day's rain runs off nowhere.

Two things follow, and both matter here. Runoff grows faster than rainfall, so one 100 mm day yields far more than ten 10 mm days. And the choice of curve number moves the answer a long way: 65 to 85 roughly triples the yearly runoff. Pick it from the land, and treat the result as a range.

Rough guide, cultivated land

Sandy soil that drains fast (group A)65
Loam (group B)75
Clay loam, slow to drain (group C)82
Heavy clay, nearly sealed (group D)86

The default of 75 is loam under row crops, which is the ground around the sample map.
